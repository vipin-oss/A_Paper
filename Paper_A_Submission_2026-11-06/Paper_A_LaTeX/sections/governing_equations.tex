\section{Mathematical formulation}
\label{sec:governing}

\subsection{Geometry, fields and the variational principle}
\label{sec:governing_variational}

Consider a transversely isotropic ($6mm$) n-type ZnO plate of thickness $d$
whose faces are the planes $z=\pm d/2$ and which occupies the half-space
$x<0$.  The plate terminates at a traction-free edge at $x=0$ and is unbounded
in $y$; a single tangential wavenumber $k_y$ is conserved because the geometry
and the material are invariant under translations in $y$.  The independent
fields are the displacement vector $u_i(\vect{x},t)$, the electrostatic
potential $\varphi(\vect{x},t)$ and the electron-concentration perturbation
$\Delta n(\vect{x},t)$ measured from the uniform doping level $n_0$.

The constitutive content of the model follows from a free-energy (enthalpy)
density that contains the strain $\varepsilon_{ij}$, its gradient
$\varepsilon_{ij,k}$, the electric field $E_i=-\partial_i\varphi$ and the carrier
perturbation $\Delta n$.  We take
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:enthalpy}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{H}\bigl(\varepsilon,\nabla\varepsilon,E,\Delta n\bigr)
={}&\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{ij}\,c_{ijkl}\,\varepsilon_{kl}
+\tfrac12\,\varepsilon_{ij,k}\,A_{ijklmn}\,\varepsilon_{lm,n}
-\tfrac12\,E_i\,\kappa_{ij}\,E_j
-e_{kij}E_k\varepsilon_{ij}\\
&-\tfrac12\,\frac{k_BT}{n_0}\,\chi^{-1}\,(\Delta n)^2
+H_{jkl}\,\Delta n\,\varepsilon_{jk,l}
-f_{ijkl}\,E_i\,\varepsilon_{jk,l},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the strain-gradient stiffness is taken in the isotropic-gradient form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:A_tensor}
A_{ijklmn}=\ell^{2}\,c_{ijkl}\,\delta_{mn},
\end{equation}
so that a single length $\ell$ measures the strain-gradient elasticity, and
$\chi$ is a dimensionless carrier-compressibility factor that is set to unity
here.  The first three terms of \eqref{eq:enthalpy} are the classical
piezoelectric enthalpy, the fourth is the strain-gradient elasticity, the fifth
is the carrier compressibility, and the last two lines contain the two
non-classical couplings.  The term $H_{jkl}\Delta n\,\varepsilon_{jk,l}$ is the
\emph{only} constitutive addition of this work; the flexoelectric term
$-f_{ijkl}E_i\varepsilon_{jk,l}$ is retained because it is part of the
established flexoelectric theory that the carrier coupling is being added to.
No other coupling, field or gradient is introduced.

Independent variations of the action with respect to $u_i$, $\varphi$ and
$\Delta n$ give, respectively, the mechanical balance, Gauss's law and the
carrier continuity equation; these are written out in
\cref{sec:governing_balance}.  The thermodynamic
conjugates that follow from \eqref{eq:enthalpy} are
\begin{align}
\sigma_{ij}&=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\varepsilon_{ij}}
=c_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}-e_{kij}E_k,
\label{eq:sigma}\\
\tau_{jkl}&=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\varepsilon_{jk,l}}
=A_{jklmn}\varepsilon_{lm,n}+H_{jkl}\Delta n-f_{ijkl}E_i,
\label{eq:tau}\\
D_i&=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial E_i}
=\kappa_{ij}E_j+e_{ijk}\varepsilon_{jk}+f_{ijkl}\varepsilon_{jk,l},
\label{eq:D}\\
\widetilde\mu&=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial\Delta n}
=\frac{k_BT}{n_0}\,\Delta n+H_{jkl}\varepsilon_{jk,l}.
\label{eq:mu}
\end{align}
Here $\sigma_{ij}$ is the symmetric Cauchy stress, $\tau_{jkl}$ is the
higher-order (hyper)stress conjugate to the strain gradient, $D_i$ is the
electric displacement and $\widetilde\mu$ is the perturbation of the
electrochemical potential of the electrons.  Equations
\eqref{eq:sigma}--\eqref{eq:mu} show the reciprocity explicitly: the same
tensor $H_{jkl}$ that shifts the hyperstress proportionally to the carrier
density also shifts the electrochemical potential proportionally to the strain
gradient.  A model that retained only one of the two would violate the
Maxwell relation $\partial^2\mathcal{H}/\partial(\Delta n)\partial
\varepsilon_{jk,l}=\partial^2\mathcal{H}/\partial\varepsilon_{jk,l}\partial
(\Delta n)$.

\subsection{Kinematics and the wave ansatz}
\label{sec:governing_kinematics}

The problem is three-dimensional in its kinematics: all three displacement
components are retained, the strain tensor is the full six-component symmetric
gradient of $\vect{u}$, and the fields depend on $x$, $y$ and $z$.  The only
reduction is the translational invariance in $y$, which allows the
Fourier ansatz
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ansatz}
\bigl\{u_i,\varphi,\Delta n\bigr\}(x,y,z,t)
=\bigl\{U_i(x,z),\Phi(x,z),N(x,z)\bigr\}\ee^{\ii(k_yy-\omega t)},
\end{equation}
with $k_y$ a real conserved wavenumber and $\omega$ a complex frequency whose
imaginary part is the temporal attenuation.  The problem that remains is a
two-dimensional boundary-value problem in $(x,z)$ for a three-component
displacement.  It is \emph{not} a plane-strain problem: the $y$ component is
retained and the strain components $\varepsilon_{yy}$, $\varepsilon_{xy}$ and
$\varepsilon_{yz}$ are all non-zero.  We therefore refer to
\eqref{eq:ansatz} as a \emph{Fourier-reduced three-dimensional} formulation and
avoid the plane-strain label, which would misdescribe the solver and would
wrongly suppress the $u_y$ branch that the edge matching uses.

For a complex $\omega$ and real $k_y$ the fields in \eqref{eq:ansatz} decay or
grow in time, and the boundary-value problem in $x$ is an eigenvalue problem in
$k_x$ of polynomial structure.  It is convenient to keep both descriptions:
the \emph{temporal} problem (real $k_x$, complex $\omega$) is used to build the
dispersion curve and the group velocity, and the \emph{spatial} problem (real
$\omega$, complex $k_x$) is used to classify the outgoing branches at the edge
and to compute the spatial attenuation $\alpha=2|\Imag k_x|$.  The two are
evaluated independently, which is what makes the comparison in
\cref{fig:fig09}(d) meaningful as a check on the implementation rather than as
an independent physical validation.

\subsection{Balance laws}
\label{sec:governing_balance}

With the conjugates \eqref{eq:sigma}--\eqref{eq:mu}, stationarity of the action
gives the mechanical balance
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:momentum}
\rho\,\ddot u_j-\partial_i\sigma_{ij}+\partial_k\partial_i\tau_{ijk}=0,
\end{equation}
Gauss's law for the mobile charge,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gauss}
\partial_iD_i=-q\,\Delta n,
\end{equation}
and the linearised carrier continuity equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:continuity}
\partial_t\bigl(q\Delta n\bigr)+\partial_iJ_i=0,
\qquad
J_i=-\frac{qDn_0}{k_BT}\,\partial_i\widetilde\mu ,
\end{equation}
where the electron flux $J_i$ is driven by the gradient of the electrochemical
potential \eqref{eq:mu}.  Equation \eqref{eq:continuity} is the standard
linearised drift--diffusion statement written in electrochemical form: expanding
\eqref{eq:mu} in \eqref{eq:continuity} recovers the familiar
$J_i=qD\,\partial_i\Delta n-q\mu_e n_0\partial_i\varphi$ with the Einstein
relation $D=\mu_e k_BT/q$.  Using $\widetilde\mu$ instead of $(\Delta n,\varphi)$
is what allows the $H$ coupling to enter as a single additive term rather than
as two coupled ones.  In the harmonic regime \eqref{eq:continuity} becomes
$-\ii\omega q\Delta n+\partial_iJ_i=0$, which is the form implemented in the
solver.

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{sec:governing_bc}

The boundary conditions follow from the surface terms of the variational
principle and are stated here for the three distinct surfaces of the problem.
This is the point that must be described exactly, because the physical meaning
of the electrical conditions is easy to invert.

\paragraph{Plate faces $z=\pm d/2$.}
The faces are free surfaces: the traction and the higher-order double traction
vanish,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_faces_mech}
\sigma_{iz}=0,\qquad \tau_{ijz}=0,\qquad z=\pm d/2,
\end{equation}
the electrons cannot leave the crystal (blocking contacts, no surface
recombination),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_faces_carrier}
J_z=0,\qquad z=\pm d/2,
\end{equation}
and the electrical condition is one of the two following alternatives.
\emph{Grounded} (short-circuit, perfectly conducting electrodes covering both
faces, the plate shorted to itself):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_grounded}
\varphi=0,\qquad z=\pm d/2 .
\end{equation}
\emph{Open circuit} (no electrodes, no free charge on the surfaces): the
variation of $\varphi$ at the face is free, so the natural condition is that the
normal electric displacement vanishes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_open}
D_z=0,\qquad z=\pm d/2 .
\end{equation}
Equation \eqref{eq:bc_grounded} is an \emph{essential} condition and is imposed
on the basis functions; \eqref{eq:bc_open} is a \emph{natural} condition and is
enforced weakly.  Earlier versions of this manuscript interchanged the two
labels; the solver has always imposed $\varphi=0$ for the grounded case and
$D_z=0$ for the open case, and the labels used here are the ones the code
implements.

In the open-circuit case the potential is determined by \eqref{eq:gauss} only
up to an additive constant, because adding a constant to $\varphi$ leaves every
field invariant.  That constant is a genuine null vector of the electrostatic
operator.  It is removed by imposing a zero-thickness-mean gauge on the
potential basis.  This is a gauge choice, not a physical effect: no field
gradient, no charge density and no power flux is altered by it.  It is
nevertheless the reason why the open-circuit system is solvable at all, and it
is reported as such rather than as a validation of the open-circuit response.

\paragraph{Reflecting edge $x=0$.}
The edge is a free surface in vacuum.  Traction-free and double-traction-free
conditions hold,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_edge_mech}
\sigma_{ix}=0,\qquad \tau_{ijx}=0,\qquad x=0,
\end{equation}
no free charge leaves through the edge, so the normal electric displacement
vanishes,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_edge_elec}
D_x=0,\qquad x=0,
\end{equation}
and the carriers are blocked at the edge,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bc_edge_carrier}
J_x=0,\qquad x=0 .
\end{equation}
The edge conditions \eqref{eq:bc_edge_mech}--\eqref{eq:bc_edge_carrier} are the
homogeneous conditions that the outgoing-mode matching satisfies mode by mode.

\paragraph{Radiation towards $x\to-\infty$.}
The solution must consist of an incoming guided mode, an outgoing
backward-propagating mode and a finite set of outgoing evanescent and
propagating branches.  No wave may arrive from $x=-\infty$ other than the
prescribed incident one.  This is the only condition imposed at infinity and it
is what makes the problem a scattering problem rather than an eigenvalue
problem.

\subsection{Power flux and the energy identity}
\label{sec:governing_energy}

The mechanical, electrical and chemical power fluxes in the $x$ direction are
combined into the single quantity
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:powerflux}
P_x(z)=\tfrac12\Real\Bigl[-\sigma_{xi}\,\dot u_i^{*}
-\tau_{xjk}\,\dot\varepsilon_{jk}^{*}
+\varphi\,\dot D_x^{*}
+\widetilde\mu\,\dot\Gamma_x^{*}\Bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_x$ is the $x$ component of the carrier flux and the dot denotes
the time derivative; the four terms are the elastic, hyperelastic, dielectric
and chemical contributions.  Its integral over the thickness is the power the
mode carries across a plane $x=\text{const}$.  In the harmonic regime the
thickness-integrated flux is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Px_int}
\mathcal{P}_x=\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}P_x(z)\,\dd z ,
\end{equation}
and it is this quantity, and not the magnitude of the wavevector, that decides
whether a mode is backward.

For a scattering solution the following identity holds exactly and is used as
the primary numerical check:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energy_identity}
\underbrace{P_{\text{inc}}}_{\text{incident}}
+\underbrace{\sum_{j}P_j}_{\text{outgoing self-powers}}
+\underbrace{P_{\text{cross}}}_{\text{interference}}
=\underbrace{P_{\text{edge}}-P_{\text{diss}}}_{\text{flux at the plane minus dissipation}} .
\end{equation}
The terms of \eqref{eq:energy_identity} are not independent energy channels.
$P_j$ are the \emph{signed} self-powers of the individual outgoing modes, so
that $-P_j>0$ for a mode carrying energy away from the edge; $P_{\text{cross}}$
is the bilinear interference between them, which is non-zero whenever the
modes are not mutually orthogonal in the power metric; and $P_{\text{diss}}$
collects the conduction loss inside the control volume,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dissipation}
P_{\text{diss}}=\tfrac12\alpha\int\!\!\int
\Bigl[(k_x^2+k_y^2)|\widetilde\mu|^2+|\partial_z\widetilde\mu|^2\Bigr]\dd z ,
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=D/(d\,c_s)$ the dimensionless diffusivity.  Only the propagating
modes of $\{P_j\}$ transport energy to infinity; the evanescent members are
reactive at the plane and are reported separately, never as transported power.
The residual of \eqref{eq:energy_identity} is what the solver minimises and
what \cref{fig:fig05}(c) plots.

\subsection{Non-dimensionalisation and the dimensionless groups}
\label{sec:governing_nondim}

The solver works in the variables of \cref{tab:groups}: lengths are scaled by
$d$, stresses by $c_{44}$, time by $d/c_s$ with the shear speed
$c_s=\sqrt{c_{44}/\rho}$, the carrier perturbation by $n_0$ and the potential by
the Debye scale.  Two derived quantities appear repeatedly: the thermal energy
per carrier divided by the doping,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:a_scale}
a=\frac{k_BT}{n_0}=\frac{qD}{\mu_e n_0},
\end{equation}
and the Debye length $L_D=\sqrt{\kappa_{33}k_BT/(q^2n_0)}$.  With these
choices the model is governed by five dimensionless numbers.

The first group deserves a comment because it controls how much of the carrier
physics is visible at all.  Since $Q=d/L_D=0.075<1$, the plate is thinner than
the Debye length at the chosen doping: the screening is partial, the carrier
perturbation is not confined to a thin surface layer, and the semiconductor
coupling therefore acts throughout the thickness.  This is consistent with the
observation of Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2025pscplate} that the semiconductor
coupling of a piezoelectric plate is active only inside a window of carrier
concentrations; outside that window the plate behaves as a piezoelectric or
elastic one.  The coupling used in the sweeps is
$H/H^{*}=\eta$ with $\eta\in[-0.05,0.05]$ and
$H^{*}=\SI{4.0097e-22}{\joule\metre}$, i.e.\
$|H|\le\SI{2.0e-23}{\joule\metre}$.

\subsection{Thermodynamic admissibility of the added coupling}
\label{sec:governing_stability}

The enthalpy \eqref{eq:enthalpy} is a quadratic form in
$(\varepsilon,\nabla\varepsilon,E,\Delta n)$ and is positive definite only if
the carrier and strain-gradient blocks are not over-coupled.  Eliminating
$\Delta n$ blockwise from \eqref{eq:enthalpy} gives the Schur-complement
condition
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:schur}
\frac{H^2\,(c^{-1})_{33}}{\ell^{2}\,a}<1 ,
\end{equation}
which is the statement that the coupled stiffness of the plate remains positive
for every strain-gradient field.  The solver enforces \eqref{eq:schur}
explicitly and refuses to run outside it.  The value $H^{*}$ quoted above is the
root of \eqref{eq:schur} at the operating parameters, so the five levels used in
the sweeps correspond to $H/H^{*}\in[-0.05,0.05]$ and satisfy
\eqref{eq:schur} with a margin of a factor of twenty.  All reported solutions
therefore sit inside a strictly convex enthalpy; a coupling large enough to
violate \eqref{eq:schur} would signal a constitutive instability of the model
rather than a physical effect.
